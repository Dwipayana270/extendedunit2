Section 1

Representing Linear Inequalities

The endpoint symbol tells you whether the boundary is included:

SymbolMeaningEndpoint
<Less thanOpen circle
>Greater thanOpen circle
$\le$Less than or equalClosed circle
$\ge$Greater than or equalClosed circle

AND: $2 < x \le 5$ (both must hold).

OR: $x < -1$ or $x \ge 3$ (either may hold).

Solve $-3 \le 2x + 1 < 7$:

  1. $-4 \le 2x < 6$.
  2. $-2 \le x < 3$.

Graphing on the coordinate plane

  1. Replace the inequality with $=$.
  2. Solid line for $\le, \ge$; dashed line for $<, >$.
  3. Shade the correct region.
  4. Test a point such as $(0, 0)$.

Draw a dashed line $y = 2x + 1$. Test $(0, 0)$: $0 > 1$ is false, so shade the side away from $(0, 0)$ — above the line.

Graph of Example 2 — dashed boundary y = 2x + 1: the test point (0, 0) fails, so the region above the line is shaded.

Guided practice

  1. Write the inequality represented by a closed circle at $5$ shading to the left.
  2. Sketch $y \le -\frac{1}{2}x + 3$ and test $(0, 0)$.

Solid boundary = boundary points are solutions. Dashed = boundary not included. Do not mix these up.

$(0, 0)$ is the easiest test unless it lies on the boundary — then pick another, like $(1, 1)$ or $(0, 1)$.

Section 2

Graphing Systems of Linear Inequalities

A system of inequalities is solved by finding the region that satisfies all of them at once.

  1. Graph each inequality one at a time.
  2. Find the overlap of all shaded regions.
  3. The overlap is the feasible region.

Graph $y \ge 0$, $x \ge 0$, $x + y \le 6$, $y \le x + 2$.

The feasible region is the polygon bounded by all four lines. Every point inside it satisfies every inequality.

Example 3 — y ≥ 0, x ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 6, y ≤ x + 2: the green overlap is the feasible region.

Guided practice

  1. Sketch and shade $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, $2x + y \le 8$.
  2. Add $y \ge 2$ to your sketch. Which points remain feasible?

Constraints like $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$ are easy to forget, but they limit the solution to the first quadrant — usually required in real contexts.

The solution is the overlap, not the union. Points satisfying only some constraints are not feasible.

Section 3

Identifying Feasible Regions

Properties of a feasible region: it may be bounded or unbounded, and its vertices occur where boundary lines intersect.

Finding vertices:

  1. Identify pairs of boundary lines.
  2. Solve them simultaneously.
  3. Check each intersection lies in the feasible region.
  4. List the valid vertices.

For $y \ge 0$, $x \ge 0$, $x + y \le 6$, $y \le x + 2$, the vertices are $(0, 0)$, $(0, 2)$, $(2, 4)$, $(6, 0)$.

Example 4 — the same system with all four corner vertices marked.

$x + y \le 2$ and $x + y \ge 5$ cannot both hold: no point belongs to both shaded regions, so no feasible region exists.

Example 5 — x + y ≤ 2 (blue) and x + y ≥ 5 (orange) never overlap: no feasible region.

$x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, $x + y \ge 4$ gives a feasible but unbounded region. A maximum may not exist unless extra constraints are added.

Example 6 — x, y ≥ 0 with x + y ≥ 4: feasible but unbounded, so a maximum may not exist.

Guided practice

  1. Let $x \ge 1$, $y \ge 0$, $x + y \le 8$. Find all vertices and check one by substitution.
  2. Explain why $y \le 2$ and $y \ge 5$ together give no feasible region.

Even if two lines intersect, discard that vertex if it fails one of the other inequalities.

With an unbounded region, carefully ask whether the optimum exists before trusting an answer.

Section 4

Solving Linear Programming Problems

Components of an LP problem:

ComponentDescription
Objective functionWhat to maximise or minimise
ConstraintsInequalities
Feasible regionAll points satisfying the constraints
Optimal solutionPoint giving the max/min value

If an optimal value exists, it occurs at a vertex of the feasible region. Evaluate the objective only at the vertices.

Graphical method

  1. Define variables.
  2. Write the objective function.
  3. Write all constraints.
  4. Graph the constraints and identify the feasible region.
  5. Find all vertices.
  6. Evaluate the objective at each vertex.
  7. Select the optimal value.
  8. Interpret.

Two products A and B. A needs 2 h machining and 1 h finishing; B needs 1 h machining and 3 h finishing. Available: 16 h machining, 24 h finishing. Profit: \$30 per A, \$40 per B.

Let $x$ = number of A, $y$ = number of B.

Objective: $P = 30x + 40y$.

Constraints: $2x + y \le 16$, $x + 3y \le 24$, $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$.

Vertices: $(0, 0)$, $(8, 0)$, $(4.8, 6.4)$, $(0, 8)$.

Vertex$P$
$(0, 0)$0
$(8, 0)$240
$(4.8, 6.4)$400
$(0, 8)$320

Maximum profit $= \$400$ at $x = 4.8$, $y = 6.4$.

Example 7 — factory optimisation: the dashed objective line 30x + 40y = 400 sits on the optimum (4.8, 6.4).

Solve the intersecting boundary lines exactly:

  1. From $2x + y = 16$: $y = 16 - 2x$.
  2. Substitute into $x + 3y = 24$: $x + 3(16 - 2x) = 24$.
  3. $-5x = -24 \Rightarrow x = 4.8$.
  4. $y = 6.4$.

For larger systems, use matrices or the simplex method.

If whole units are required, test integer points near the continuous optimum:

PointCheck constraintsProfit
$(5, 6)$10 + 6 = 16, 5 + 18 = 23390
$(4, 6)$8 + 6 = 14, 4 + 18 = 22360
$(3, 7)$6 + 7 = 13, 3 + 21 = 24370
$(0, 8)$0 + 8 = 8, 0 + 24 = 24320

Best integer solution: $(5, 6)$, profit \$390.

Guided practice

  1. A baker has 20 kg flour and 15 kg sugar. A cake needs 2 kg flour and 1 kg sugar; a loaf needs 1 kg flour and 1.5 kg sugar. Cakes sell for \$8, loaves for \$6. Set up the LP and find the best integer mix. define your variables clearly.

Sketching well but forgetting one vertex changes the answer. Solve every boundary-line intersection before evaluating.

In most real problems $x$ and $y$ count whole products. Rounding the continuous optimum up or down can violate constraints — test neighbouring integer points.

Section 5

Interpreting Optimal Solutions

A finished LP answer is not a coordinate pair — it is a report. Include the decision variables, the optimal value, the real-world meaning, and assumptions or limitations.

Checklist for an optimal solution

  • Are there integer restrictions?
  • Is the feasible region bounded?
  • Does the answer make practical sense?
  • identify any alternative optimal solutions (a flat edge of the region).
  • State the assumptions and limitations of the model.

For the factory example in Section 4: "Make 5 units of A and 6 units of B for a maximum profit of \$390. This assumes hourly capacities, linear profits, and that fractional units are not allowed. The model ignores set-up times and market demand."

Guided practice

  1. Reinterpret your baker's optimum: state the decision, the profit, and one limitation of the model.

"$(5, 6)$ gives \$390" is a calculation. "Make 5 cakes and 6 loaves for \$390" is an interpretation — that is what criteria A and C reward.

If two adjacent vertices give the same optimum, every point on the edge between them is also optimal.

Section 6

Analysing & Using Well-Defined Procedures for Optimisation

Optimisation is a fixed, repeatable procedure. Following it in order removes most errors.

Standard procedure

  1. Understand the problem.
  2. Define variables with units.
  3. Write the objective function.
  4. Write all constraints.
  5. Include non-negativity constraints.
  6. Graph accurately.
  7. Identify the feasible region.
  8. Find all vertices.
  9. Evaluate the objective at each vertex.
  10. Select the optimal value.
  11. Interpret in context.

Common errors

  • Forgetting non-negativity constraints.
  • Shading the wrong side.
  • Missing a vertex.
  • Not checking practical sense.
  • Confusing max/min with the constraints themselves.

Guided practice

  1. Reopen your bakery model and walk the full 11-step procedure aloud, checking off each step.
  2. Find one error you made in an earlier LP attempt and justify the corrected step.

In optimisation, a fixed algorithm beats guessing. The vertices always hold the answer — trust the method.

Defining variables with units (kg, hours, \$) prevents the most common modelling mistakes.
